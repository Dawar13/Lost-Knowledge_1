\title{Known but Unsayable: Decoding Defaults, Not Forgetting,\\ Hide What Language Models Know}

\author{Anonymous ACL submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Language models are said to lose the long tail of knowledge. We separate knowledge a model lacks, holds but rarely says, and holds but cannot say because the sampler gives it probability exactly zero. Top-p, top-k and min-p sampling delete low-ranked tokens, so one deleted token makes a name impossible. We pair an exact per-token survival test from prior work with a recognition test against near-miss foils, run each deployed sampler exactly, with a numeric margin; \ptSamplesTotal{} real samples never contradict it. Of the long-tail entities Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct recognises, \gzQwenInstPnine{} can never be produced under top-p 0.9 and \gzQwenInstShipped{} under its shipped sampler, against \gzQwenBasePnine{} for its base model. Post-training buries the tail and truncation seals it. Across OLMo-2's post-training stages the share rises from \goRbuOlmoBase{} to \goRbuOlmoInst{}, mostly at supervised fine-tuning, and the cut moves to the first token. Without truncation, the median deleted name would still be \ztMedRatioQ{} times less likely than the base model's. Truncation adds a hard ceiling: across \ptCategories{} categories, no amount of sampling finds more than \ztPlateauQI{} of \ztRecQI{} recognised names, so sample-based knowledge estimates measure that ceiling. Rarer entities are deleted more often. Relaxing truncation at the first token recovers only \pfRecoveredQwen{}; we report the frontier against invented answers.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Ask Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct \citep{qwen2024qwen25} for a capital city of a U.S. state \cwShippedSamples{} times under the sampler it ships with, and it gives \cwShippedDistinct{} different answers: \cwAnsNameA{} (\cwAnsCountA{} times), \cwAnsNameB{} (\cwAnsCountB{}), \cwAnsNameC{} (\cwAnsCountC{}) and \cwAnsNameD{} (\cwAnsCountD{}). It knows many more. Shown a state capital beside three large U.S. cities that are not capitals, it picks the capital for \cwCapsMc{} of the \gzCapitalsMembers{} capitals. It cannot say them. At the first step of the answer, nucleus sampling with $p=0.9$ keeps only \cwInstKeptPnine{} candidate first tokens; the base model keeps \cwBaseKeptPnine{}. \cwFirstExName{} starts with a token ranked \cwFirstExRank{}, behind tokens that together hold \cwFirstExMass{} of the probability, so it can never begin. \cwLaterExName{} shares its first token with \cwLaterExRival{}. After that token the model puts \cwLaterExMass{} of the probability on \cwLaterExRival{}'s next token, so even $p=0.95$ never completes \cwLaterExName{}. Under top-$p$ 0.9 the base model can reach \gzCapitalsReachBase{} of the \gzCapitalsMembers{} capitals and the chat model \gzCapitalsReachInst{} (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}).

The mechanism is general. Nucleus (top-$p$) sampling \citep{holtzman2020curious}, top-$k$ sampling \citep{fan2018hierarchical} and min-$p$ \citep{nguyen2025minp} keep only the most likely tokens at each step and delete the rest before sampling. If one token of a name falls outside the kept set at any step, the probability of the whole name is exactly zero. No number of samples, retries or self-consistency votes can produce it. Whether this happens can be tested exactly, with one teacher-forced pass per answer; \citet{awad2026lost} introduced this per-token survival test for ordinary words in book continuations.

This matters for the debate on homogenisation and knowledge collapse \citep{peterson2025collapse,wright2026epistemic}. When a model never produces a rare fact, three different things can be true: the model does not store it; it stores it but gives it a small probability, so enough samples will find it; or it stores it and the sampler deletes it. In practice the second and third cases combine. Post-training pushes most recognised names to tiny probabilities, and truncation then sets them to exactly zero: post-training buries the tail, and truncation seals it. The burial is a property of the model; the seal is a property of a decoding setting. From samples, both look like ignorance. The seal also has a cost downstream. Text written under these samplers can never contain such names, and training on generated text is known to erode the tails of a distribution \citep{shumailov2024collapse,guo2024curious}.

We measure the seal exactly and the burial beneath it. Our contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Recognised but unreachable.} We pair the per-token survival test of \citet{awad2026lost} with a recognition test against near-miss foils. We run each model's deployed processor chain exactly and add a calibrated numeric margin. Real samples never contradict a robust verdict: \ptViolationsTotal{} violations in \ptSamplesTotal{} samples at full scale (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item \textbf{How much, at deployed settings.} Among entities Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct recognises, \gzQwenInstShipped{} of the pilot entities are unreachable under its shipped sampler, against \gzQwenBasePnine{} for its base model at top-$p$ 0.9. The result holds on the categories where the recognition test is strongest and under the stricter all-wordings count (Section~\ref{sec:howmuch}).
\item \textbf{Where it comes from.} Post-training is known to concentrate answers \citep{jang2026instruction,parikh2026census}. We link that concentration to exact zeros, stage by stage: from OLMo-2's base model through three post-training stages, most of the rise happens at supervised fine-tuning, and the cut moves to the first token of the name (Section~\ref{sec:where}).
\item \textbf{What it does to measurement.} Truncation caps what any amount of sampling can find at the reachable set. Sample-based knowledge estimators therefore see a ceiling, and below it a distribution that buries most recognised names. We split the gap to the recognised set into these two parts, using log-probabilities whose predictions match real samples (Section~\ref{sec:measure}).
\item \textbf{Who loses, and what a fix costs.} Rarer recognised entities are deleted more often. Relaxing truncation at the first token recovers little, because names fail again later; we report the frontier between reachable knowledge and invented answers, with practical as well as formal recovery (Section~\ref{sec:fix}). \pdSelfCheckFix{}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Exact reachability}\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Samplers.} At each decoding step a sampler applies, in the order used by Hugging Face \texttt{transformers} 5.22.2: repetition penalty, temperature $T$, top-$k$, top-$p$ and min-$p$. Each step sets some logits to $-\infty$; sampling draws from the softmax of the rest. A token is \emph{kept} if its processed logit is finite. Other inference engines order these steps differently (Limitations).

\paragraph{Keep rules.} For a target token $t$ with logit $z_t$: top-$k$ keeps $t$ iff fewer than $k$ tokens have a larger logit; top-$p$ keeps $t$ iff $G(t)<p$, where $G(t)$ is the probability, at temperature $T$, of the tokens strictly more likely than $t$; min-$p$ keeps $t$ iff its probability is at least $\textit{min-p}$ times the top token's. Unit tests on random logits check that these rules agree with the library's processors.

\paragraph{Reachability of an answer.} Write an entity $e$ after prompt $x$ as tokens $t_1\ldots t_L$ (the tokenisation the model itself produces in context). $e$ is reachable under sampler $S$ iff every $t_j$ is kept given $x$ and $t_{<j}$. This is the per-token survival test of \citet{awad2026lost}. One teacher-forced pass gives every $t_j$'s logits, so reachability is exact and needs no sampling. It also yields critical values: $p^*(e)=\max_j G_j(e)$ ($e$ is reachable under top-$p$ iff $p>p^*$), $k^*(e)$ and the critical min-$p$. For combined samplers (the shipped settings) we run the library's own processor chain at every position. An entity is reachable if any of its surface forms (label or up to three aliases) is.

\paragraph{A numeric margin.} The same logits computed two ways (a batched teacher-forced pass, and key-value-cached generation) differ slightly in bfloat16, and bfloat16 logits often tie. A token on a truncation boundary can therefore be kept by one computation and cut by the other. We call $e$ \emph{robustly unreachable} if some token stays cut even after its logit is raised by $\epsilon$ relative to every other token. Every processor above is monotone in this shift, and the shift breaks all ties. $\epsilon$ is calibrated per model before each experiment. We sample \gzCalPerCategory{} answers per pilot category under each of two samplers (\gzCalSamples{} answers, \gzCalSeqs{} distinct), compute for each the logit shift at which its verdict would flip, and take the smallest value on a fixed grid that covers every sampled answer (Appendix~\ref{app:margin}). For Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, $\epsilon=\gzEps$; the largest relative logit gap among the tokens that decide a verdict was \gzGapMax{} logits. Headline numbers use the robust verdict, a lower bound; exact and upper-bound values are reported beside them (Appendix~\ref{app:margin}).

\paragraph{Validation.} We draw 1{,}000 real samples per category from \texttt{generate()} under each claimed sampler and check that no answer we call robustly unreachable is ever produced. On the pilot there were \gzViolations{} violations in \gzSamples{} samples for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and \goViolations{} in \goSamples{} for the OLMo-2 post-trained stages. On the full TailCat there were \ptViolationsQwenInst{} in \ptSamplesQwenInst{} samples for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and \ptViolationsOlmoInst{} in \ptSamplesOlmoInst{} for OLMo-2-Instruct. For Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct on CPU in float32, the two computations agree exactly and every sampled answer is scored reachable; we did not repeat this check for the 7B models.

\section{TailCat and recognition}\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Categories.} TailCat pairs categories of long-tail entities with near-miss foils from sibling categories, built from Wikidata \citep{vrandecic2014wikidata}: for example poets who wrote in Tamil (foils: Telugu and Kannada poets) or rivers in Kerala (foils: rivers in Karnataka and Tamil Nadu). Members are stratified over popularity (Wikipedia sitelinks) and must have articles in at least two languages, which removes single-wiki bot imports whose transliterated labels no reader could know. The pilot has 11 categories (\gzEntities{} entities) including a head control, US state capitals. The full TailCat crosses 14 entity types (people, places, works and organisations) with 24 countries, 17 of them in South Asia, Africa, Southeast Asia and Latin America, and adds writers in 12 Indian languages. Foils come from the same type in neighbouring or easily confused countries. We keep a category only if it yields at least 30 members and 30 foils: \ptCategories{} categories and \ptMembers{} sampled members (at most 150 per category).

\paragraph{Recognition.} An entity counts as recognised if the model (i) answers ``Yes'' to `Is ``$e$'' \emph{category}?' with a score $V(e)=P(\text{Yes})/(P(\text{Yes})+P(\text{No}))$ at or above the 95th percentile of the category's foils, so at most 5\% of foils pass, and (ii) picks $e$ out of three foils in a four-way choice. RBU is the share of recognised entities that are unreachable. The yes/no probe separates members from foils with AUROC \gzQwenInstAuroc{} for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct on the pilot and \ptAurocQwenInst{} on the full TailCat, below the 0.85 we set in advance (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}). Two checks follow. On the \rcPilotQwenPassN{} pilot categories where the probe reaches 0.85 on its own, RBU at top-$p$ 0.9 is \rcPilotQwenInstPass{} for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and \rcPilotQwenBasePass{} for its base model; on the \rcFullQwenPassN{} such categories of the full TailCat it is \rcFullQwenInstPass{} and \rcFullQwenBasePass{}. With a second probe wording that adds worked examples, \rcProbeTwoRecognised{} pilot entities are recognised and RBU is \rcProbeTwoQwenInst{} and \rcProbeTwoQwenBase{} (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}). A third test asks the model for each name directly, with a question only that name answers: \pdAskDirectly{}.

\section{Where it comes from}\label{sec:where}

\paragraph{Post-training, stage by stage.} OLMo-2 \citep{olmo2024olmo2} releases its base model and each post-training stage. RBU at top-$p$ 0.9 is \goRbuOlmoBase{} for the base model, \goRbuOlmoSft{} after supervised fine-tuning, \goRbuOlmoDpo{} after preference tuning and \goRbuOlmoInst{} for the final model. Most of the rise happens at supervised fine-tuning. Among entities recognised by both, \goPairedInstOnly{} are unreachable only for the final model and \goPairedBaseOnly{} only for the base model (sign test, $z=\goPairedZOlmo$, $p$\goPairedPOlmo{}); for Qwen2.5 the counts are \gzPairedInstOnly{} and \gzPairedBaseOnly{} ($z=\goPairedZQwen$, $p$\goPairedPQwen{}). Neither fine-tuning objective can set a probability to zero by itself: the KL-regularised preference optimum keeps the support of the reference model \citep{rafailov2023dpo}, and cross-entropy training keeps logits finite. What they change is where the mass goes (Figure~\ref{fig:stages}). They bury the tail: on the full TailCat, the median recognised name that top-$p$ 0.9 deletes from Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct would have probability \ztMedQQI{} without truncation, against \ztMedQQB{} for the median name the base model deletes (\ztMedQOI{} and \ztMedQOB{} for OLMo-2).

\paragraph{The first token.} Post-training is known to concentrate the answer slot: instruction-tuned models give one answer where a distribution is wanted \citep{jang2026instruction}, category prompts such as ``Name a tree'' converge across many models \citep{parikh2026census}, and output diversity collapses at identifiable post-training stages \citep{karouzos2026diversity}. Our test shows what this does to recognised knowledge. The share of unreachable recognised entities whose first failing token is the first token of the name rises from \goFirstTokOlmoBase{} (OLMo-2 base) to \goFirstTokOlmoInst{} (OLMo-2-Instruct), and from \gzQwenBaseFirstTok{} to \gzQwenInstFirstTok{} for Qwen2.5. The mean probability of the single most likely first answer token rises with it, from \goTopOneOlmoBase{} to \goTopOneOlmoInst{} (OLMo-2) and from \goTopOneQwenBase{} to \goTopOneQwenInst{} (Qwen2.5). Across \goSpearmanN{} (model, category) points, first-token concentration and RBU have Spearman correlation \goSpearman{}. This pooled value mixes the gap between base and chat models with the association inside each model. A post hoc check separates the two. Within each of the four models, over the \ptCategories{} full-TailCat categories, the Spearman correlation is between \ctMechWithinScaleMin{} and \ctMechWithinScaleMax{}, with every 95\% CI above zero. Within a category, across OLMo-2's stages, the Pearson correlation is \ctMechStagesFE{} (\ctMechStagesFECI{}). The association is real but weaker than the pooled value suggests. Appendix~\ref{app:walk} shows real names token by token: the instruct model rules them out at the first token, while the base model starts them and loses them later.

\paragraph{Mass and rank.} For OLMo-2, top-$k$ RBU barely moves across stages (\goKfiftyOlmoBase{} to \goKfiftyOlmoInst{} at $k=50$) while top-$p$ RBU rises steeply: post-training changed how much probability the head takes more than the order of the candidates. On the full TailCat, too, OLMo-2-Instruct sits close to the base models under top-$k$ and far above them under top-$p$ and min-$p$ (Figure~\ref{fig:rbu_curves}). Qwen2.5 does not show this pattern. Its top-$k$ 50 RBU rises from \goKfiftyQwenBase{} to \goKfiftyQwenInst{}, so its post-training also reorders candidates. Truncation by mass (top-$p$, min-$p$) is the larger effect in both families, but not the only one.

\section{What it does to measurement}\label{sec:measure}

Let $q_S(e)$ be the probability that one sample under sampler $S$ begins with entity $e$. After $N$ samples the expected number of distinct entities seen is $\sum_e[1-(1-q_S(e))^N]$, which approaches the size of the reachable set $\{e:q_S(e)>0\}$ as $N$ grows. Unseen-species estimators, from Good-Turing to Chao1 \citep{chao1984nonparametric} and ACE \citep{chao1992coverage}, aim at that support size. Applied to truncated samples they can at best recover the reachable set, never the recognised set. Truncation thus sets a ceiling. Below it, a sampler may still find few names, because the distribution itself puts little mass on them. \citet{li2025unseen} observed that nucleus sampling lowers their knowledge estimates and sampled without it; the ceiling explains why, and top-$k$ and the shipped chains impose one too.

\paragraph{Zero or merely tiny?} Without truncation, a deleted name has a small but positive probability. In a post hoc analysis we compute it from the stored teacher-forced log-probabilities. This reading is well calibrated. In \ztCalSamplesQI{} untruncated pilot samples per model, the models gave \ztCalObsQI{} member answers against \ztCalPredQI{} predicted (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) and \ztCalObsOI{} against \ztCalPredOI{} (OLMo-2-Instruct). For most deleted names the probability is tiny. On the full TailCat, the median recognised name that top-$p$ 0.9 deletes from Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct has probability \ztMedQQI{}; seeing it once would take about \ztMedSightQI{} samples. Only \ztSeenKQI{} of the deleted names would appear in 1{,}000 untruncated samples, and \ztSeenMQI{} in a million. The burial comes from post-training (Section~\ref{sec:where}); truncation seals it, and its cost is the ceiling. Under top-$p$ 0.9 no amount of sampling finds more than \ztPlateauQI{} of the \ztRecQI{} recognised names across the \ztPromptsQI{} prompts. At 1{,}000 samples per prompt, truncated sampling finds between \ztTruncKLoQI{} and \ztTruncKHiQI{} of them in expectation, and untruncated sampling \ztUntrKQI{}. At a million samples per prompt the truncated count stays at or below \ztTruncMHiQI{}, while untruncated sampling reaches \ztUntrMQI{}; it passes the ceiling after about \ztNstarQI{} samples per prompt. Under the shipped sampler the ceiling is \ztShipPlateauQI{} names, and untruncated sampling at the same temperature passes it after about \ztShipNstarQI{} samples per prompt. This last calculation omits the repetition penalty, so it is approximate.

\paragraph{The estimator gap, split.} With 1{,}000 samples per category under Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct's shipped sampler on the full TailCat, the samples name \peQwenShippedSobs{} distinct members. The species set is our capped member list (at most 150 per category). Chao1 estimates \peQwenShippedChao{} and ACE \peQwenShippedAce{}, while \peQwenShippedReach{} members are reachable, so at this sample size Chao1 recovers \peQwenShippedChaoShare{} of the reachable set. The model recognises \peQwenShippedRecog{} members, and truncation seals \peQwenShippedTrunc{} of them exactly. Without truncation, the same number of samples would find about \ztShipUntrKQI{} recognised names (approximate, as above), where truncation caps it at \ztShipPlateauQI{}. The gap between \peQwenShippedChao{} and \peQwenShippedRecog{} is therefore mostly concentration and partly truncation (Figure~\ref{fig:estimators}). Under top-$p$ 0.9 the samples name \peQwenPnineSobs{} members, Chao1 estimates \peQwenPnineChao{}, \peQwenPnineReach{} members are reachable and \peQwenPnineRecog{} recognised.

Reachability is computed for the canonical tokenisation of the listed name forms. A few sampled members fall outside the computed reachable set: \peQwenShippedOutside{} under the shipped sampler and \peQwenPnineOutside{} under top-$p$ 0.9 for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, and \peOlmoPnineOutside{} for OLMo-2-Instruct. In the cases we inspected the model wrote the name through a different tokenisation, or as a case variant of a listed form (``Universidad de los Andes'' for the listed ``Universidad de Los Andes''). No sample matched the token sequence of a robustly unreachable answer.

\section{Who loses}\label{sec:who}

\paragraph{Popularity.} We fit a logistic model of unreachability on $\log_{10}(1+\text{sitelinks})$ for recognised entities, with generalised estimating equations clustered by category \citep{liang1986longitudinal}. For Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct each tenfold increase in Wikipedia language coverage multiplies the odds of being unreachable by \ptOrUnreachQwenInst{} (95\% CI \ptOrUnreachQwenInstLo{} to \ptOrUnreachQwenInstHi{}); for OLMo-2-Instruct by \ptOrUnreachOlmoInst{}. The same model for recognition among all members gives \ptOrRecogQwenInst{} for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. Figure~\ref{fig:popularity} (Appendix~\ref{app:breakdowns}) shows the same pattern by popularity bin: unreachability falls steadily with popularity, while recognition rises only in the most popular bin. Rarer names also tend to have more tokens, and so more chances to be cut. In a post hoc check that adds the label's token count and entity-type fixed effects, the odds ratio is \ctPopOrQwenControlled{} (\ctPopOrQwenControlledCI{}) for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and \ctPopOrOlmoControlled{} for OLMo-2-Instruct, and each extra token raises the odds by \ctLabelTokenPctMin{} to \ctLabelTokenPctMax{}. Under Qwen's shipped sampler the popularity effect is stronger still (odds ratio \ctPopOrQwenShipped{} without controls, \ctPopOrQwenShippedControlled{} with them).

\paragraph{Regions and types.} Appendix~\ref{app:breakdowns} breaks RBU down by world region and entity type. RBU is not higher for categories from South Asia, Africa or Southeast Asia than for European or North American ones; it is lower. For Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct at top-$p$ 0.9 it is \rgRbuSouthAsia{}, \rgRbuSoutheastAsia{} and \rgRbuAfrica{} for South Asian, Southeast Asian and African categories, against \rgRbuEurope{} and \rgRbuNorthAmerica{} for European and North American ones. We did not predict this. After seeing it we tested one explanation, post hoc: Western categories have more recognised members competing for the same few first tokens. That effect is real but small (odds ratio \rgCompositionOR{} per e-fold more recognised members), and controlling for it leaves \rgCompositionShareLeft{} of the gap on the log-odds scale. With composition, first-token width, popularity, name length, aliases and entity type all controlled, the odds of unreachability for these categories remain \rgGapAdjQwen{} times the Western odds (95\% CI \rgGapAdjQwenCI{}; OLMo-2-Instruct \rgGapAdjOlmo{}). The gap is already present in the base models (odds ratio \rgGapRawQwenBase{} for Qwen2.5-7B and \rgGapRawOlmoBase{} for OLMo-2), these prompts leave more names reachable per category, not only a larger share, and post-training removes a smaller share of base-reachable names there (\rgLossSouthQwen{} against \rgLossWestQwen{} for Qwen2.5, \rgLossSouthOlmo{} against \rgLossWestOlmo{} for OLMo-2). We do not know why. This is not a sign that non-Western knowledge is safe: the models recognise fewer of these names in the first place (\rgRecRateSouthAsia{} of South Asian and \rgRecRateAfrica{} of African members, against \rgRecRateEurope{} of European ones), a loss our test does not measure. We make no regional claim about who loses. The robust pattern is within categories: the rarer a recognised entity, the more likely it is unreachable.

\section{A targeted fix}\label{sec:fix}

\citet{li2025unseen} call for moving from estimating knowledge to extracting it: surfacing what a model encodes but rarely generates. If most failures happen at the first token, a natural fix is to switch truncation off for the first answer token only and keep the shipped sampler afterwards. We tested this against a sweep of standard samplers on the pilot (1{,}000 samples per category per sampler), measuring RBU, the number of distinct real members produced and the invented-name rate (answers that match no member of the category in Wikidata and no foil).

The fix fails our pre-registered bar of recovering half of the unreachable recognised entities. It recovers \pfRecoveredQwen{} for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (RBU from \pfQwenRefRbu{} to \pfQwenRelaxRbu{}, distinct members sampled from \pfQwenRefDistinct{} to \pfQwenRelaxDistinct{}, invented-name rate \pfInvDeltaQwen{} points) and \pfRecoveredOlmo{} for OLMo-2-Instruct (invented-name rate \pfInvDeltaOlmo{} points). The reason is instructive. The first failing token is usually the first token, but most names fail again later under the same sampler (as \cwLaterExName{} does in the introduction), so relaxing one position rescues only names whose every failure is at the start.

The full frontier is the more useful result (Appendix~\ref{app:frontier}). Removing truncation entirely makes every recognised entity reachable at a cost of \pfQwenUntrInv{} invented answers against \pfQwenRefInv{} under the shipped sampler for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct; top-$p$ 0.99, top-$k$ 100 and epsilon sampling lie in between (Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}). For most names this recovery is formal: they become reachable at probabilities near \ztMedQQI{} (Section~\ref{sec:measure}). The practical measure is how many distinct real members a sampler produces. In 1{,}000 samples per pilot category, untruncated sampling produced \pfQwenUntrDistinct{} distinct members against \pfQwenRefDistinct{} under the shipped sampler, and first-token relaxation \pfQwenRelaxDistinct{}. The automatic invented-name rate overstates invention: it counts real members spelled differently, written in another script or missing from Wikidata, and wrong answers, as invented. With looser matching the reference rates fall by \pfLooseDropQwen{} points for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and \pfLooseDropOlmo{} for OLMo-2-Instruct, while the differences between samplers barely change (relaxation: \pfLooseDeltaQwen{} and \pfLooseDeltaOlmo{} points). Matching labels in every language Wikidata holds gives \pdInventedMultilingual{} A hand audit of these answers is pending: \pdAuditInvented{}

A second fix keeps truncation out of the answer and lets the model check its own answers with the recognition probe, keeping only answers it endorses. It was pre-registered with the same bar; because that bar counts formal recovery, we also report distinct recognised members per 1{,}000 samples: \pdSelfCheckFix{} Generating and then verifying is an established pattern, and we claim no novelty for it. Its risk is that post-training also makes confident errors on long-tail questions far more common \citep{huang2026alignment}, and such errors may pass a model's check of its own answers.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Truncation and what it removes.} Nucleus sampling \citep{holtzman2020curious}, epsilon and eta sampling \citep{hewitt2022truncation} and min-$p$ \citep{nguyen2025minp} all give up the tail of the distribution for fluency, and top-$p$ can harm factuality \citep{lee2022factuality}. The closest work is \citet{awad2026lost}. They use the per-token survival test we also use. They ask which mid-frequency English words remain reachable in book continuations, compare seven base and aligned pairs, and tabulate shipped defaults. Aligned models erase more words in most families, by a small average margin. \citet{garcesarias2026truncation} show that human-chosen tokens often fall outside common truncation sets. We change the object and the question. We test entities the model recognises against near-miss foils, in the answer to a knowledge prompt, under the exact deployed processor chain, with a numeric margin and a check against real samples. We then ask why post-training changes reachability, stage by stage, and what follows for knowledge estimates. Appendix~\ref{app:closest} compares the two studies point by point.

\paragraph{Collapse at the answer slot.} Instruction-tuned models give one answer where a distribution is wanted, with a large gap between the top two logits at the answer position \citep{jang2026instruction}. Across 105 models, single-answer category prompts such as ``Name a tree'' converge on one answer, most strongly for heavily post-trained assistants \citep{parikh2026census}. Post-training also multiplies confident errors on long-tail factual questions \citep{huang2026alignment}. Our first-token result connects this concentration to an exact consequence: the recognised entities it pushes out of the kept set have probability zero.

\paragraph{Knowing without saying.} \citet{gekhman2025insideout} find that a model can rank the gold answer first internally and never generate it in 1{,}000 samples drawn at temperature 1, without analysing truncation; \citet{calderon2026empty} find that recall, not encoding, is the bottleneck for parametric factuality. Long-tail knowledge is harder to learn \citep{kandpal2023longtail} and to trust \citep{mallen2023trust}; a recent survey names decoding-time truncation as a possible cause without measuring it \citep{badhe2026longtail}. We add the zero-probability case on top of this low-probability one: for names that truncation seals, no amount of sampling helps.

\paragraph{Homogenisation and knowledge collapse.} Post-training reduces output diversity \citep{kirk2024understanding}; open-ended answers converge within and across models \citep{jiang2025hivemind} and across languages \citep{davey2026multilingual}; diversity collapses at identifiable post-training stages \citep{karouzos2026diversity}; and prompting can recover suppressed diversity \citep{zhang2026verbalized}. \citet{kalavasis2025limits} prove a trade-off between breadth and hallucination. \citet{peterson2025collapse} argues that models generating towards the centre of the distribution can narrow what people come to know, \citet{wright2026epistemic} measure the epistemic diversity of model claims, and training on generated text loses the tails \citep{shumailov2024collapse} and linguistic diversity \citep{guo2024curious}. These works measure diversity at a fixed decoding setting; \citet{jiang2025hivemind} find homogeneity persists under min-$p$. We separate, exactly, the part of collapse that is a decoding artefact.

\paragraph{Estimating what a model knows from samples.} \citet{li2025unseen} estimate unseen knowledge with smoothed Good-Turing and observe that nucleus sampling lowers their estimates, so they sample without it. We show that under truncation any such estimator meets a hard ceiling, the reachable set, and split the gap to the recognised set into that ceiling and the concentration beneath it, using Chao1 \citep{chao1984nonparametric} and ACE \citep{chao1992coverage} as examples.

\section*{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Recognition.} Our recognition test is a proxy. Its yes/no part separates members from near-miss foils with AUROC between \rcAurocMin{} and \rcAurocMax{} across our models, below the 0.85 we set in advance; chat models answer ``Yes'' readily to plausible foils. The four-way choice and the per-category foil threshold limit false positives, and the results hold on the categories that pass on their own and under a second probe wording (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}), but RBU is defined relative to this test. \pdAskDirectly{}

\paragraph{Prompts.} Every main result uses prompts that ask for one name. \pdListPrompt{}

\paragraph{Sampler order.} ``Shipped'' means what \texttt{generate()} in \texttt{transformers} 5.22.2 does with each model's generation configuration. Engines such as vLLM, llama.cpp and SGLang order the processors differently, so their default chains need their own check.

\paragraph{Tokenisation.} Reachability is computed for the canonical tokenisation, in context, of the listed name forms. A name can sometimes be produced through another token path or as an unlisted case variant. In the full-TailCat sample check of Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, only \ptNoncanonEntShipped{} distinct entities (shipped sampler) and \ptNoncanonEntPnine{} (top-$p$ 0.9) were ever produced solely through a non-canonical path, and in the estimator samples \peQwenShippedOutside{}, \peQwenPnineOutside{} and \peOlmoPnineOutside{} sampled members fall outside the computed reachable set (Section~\ref{sec:measure}). Canonical scoring therefore overstates RBU by a negligible amount.

\paragraph{Numerics.} Exact zero depends on how logits are computed. Our robust verdict holds for any computation within a calibrated margin of ours; implementations further away (other GPUs, quantisation) need their own calibration. The untruncated probabilities for the shipped sampler omit its repetition penalty and are approximate.

\paragraph{What truncation costs.} For most deleted names the cost of truncation is a ceiling on discovery, not lost probability mass: without truncation they would still be very unlikely. Claims about the burial beneath the ceiling rest on teacher-forced log-probabilities, which match real samples on the pilot but were not checked against samples at full scale.

\paragraph{Invented answers.} The invented-name rate is automatic. It counts spelling variants, real names missing from Wikidata and names in other scripts as invented, so the fix experiments depend on the hand audit and the multilingual matching.

\paragraph{Scope.} Two model families at 7B scale, English prompts and Wikidata categories. \pdThirdFamily{} Gemma-2 was not run, and the multilingual analysis planned for Hindi, Tamil and Bengali prompts is future work. Wikidata membership is incomplete, which affects the invented-name rate more than RBU.

\section*{Ethical considerations}

\paragraph{Risks.} Wider sampling recovers knowledge but also raises the rate of invented answers, so any fix should be paired with a check of what it produces. TailCat lists public names from Wikidata, including living people; it holds labels, aliases and sitelink counts only, and no personal data beyond what Wikidata publishes.

\section{Deviations from the pre-registered plan}\label{app:deviations}
We fixed thresholds and analyses before running the experiments and logged every later change with its reason. The changes that affect this paper:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Recognition bar.} The plan required pooled yes/no AUROC of at least 0.85 on the pilot. The pilot reached \gzQwenInstAuroc{}, and a second probe wording did not raise it. We proceeded by a recorded decision, kept the first probe as the main analysis, and report the second probe and the categories that pass on their own as checks (Section~\ref{sec:data}, Table~\ref{tab:robustness}).
\item \textbf{Head control.} The US-state-capitals control was judged on the base model, which reaches every capital; the instruct model's result is reported as a finding (Section~\ref{sec:howmuch}).
\item \textbf{Headline statistic.} The plan named RBU-all, the share unreachable under every wording, as the conservative headline. The text reports RBU-mean, which uses every wording; Table~\ref{tab:main} gives RBU-all beside it.
\item \textbf{Regression.} The plan named a mixed-effects logistic model with a random intercept per category. We fitted generalised estimating equations clustered by category instead, because our statistics library has no maximum-likelihood mixed logistic model; both account for clustering by category.
\item \textbf{Multiple comparisons.} Holm correction over the \goHolmTests{} pre-registered tests that carry $p$-values (the two base-against-final sign tests and four popularity slopes) changes no decision: the largest adjusted $p$-value is \goHolmMaxP{}.
\item \textbf{Post hoc analyses.} Four analyses were chosen after seeing results and are labelled post hoc in the text: how likely deleted names would be without truncation (Section~\ref{sec:measure}), the mechanism correlation within models and categories (Section~\ref{sec:where}), the popularity effect with length and type controls, and the region analysis (Section~\ref{sec:who}). No gate depends on them.
\item \textbf{Invented-name metric.} An internal review found sampled answers in Chinese, Japanese and Korean script, some of them real members. We therefore match labels in every language Wikidata holds. We made the change before running the second fix experiment and report the first fix under both metrics (Section~\ref{sec:fix}).
\end{itemize}
